    How the number is built

    The formula — published, not hidden

    Every read starts at 82. Deductions are grouped into clusters so one root cause is never charged five times: Capture (max −10) · Framing (max −18) · Light (max −20) · Sharpness (max −8) · Expression (max −6 on Story/Public, −12 on Dating) · Context (max −8, inference only). The result is clamped between 5 and 97 and shown as a range: base width ±5, widened when expression came from controls instead of measurement (+4), when context is unknown (+3), or when the face box is hand-marked or missing (+3). These weights are design choices whose direction comes from the research below; they have not been calibrated against human ratings, and the engine says so.

    The receipts

    • Re, Chandrasena & De Stefano (2016) — selfies are rated less attractive, less likeable and more narcissistic than other-taken photos of the same person.
    • Barry et al. (2019) — frequent selfie-posters are rated less likeable, less successful, more insecure.
    • Hinge (company data) — selfies receive roughly 40% fewer likes.
    • Photofeeler (replicated tests) — direct gaze with no smile is the weakest expression combination; a closed-mouth smile mainly buys trust.
    • Bryan (2012); Ward (2018) — close camera distance distorts features (≈30% wider nose at 12 inches) and lowers perceived trust and competence.
    • Hily survey of 5,500 US Gen Z & millennial daters — a favourite artist can be a top turn-off for about a third of daters who factor music in; polarising picks help in-group and cost cold.

    Not scored — on purpose

    Backwards caps, sunglasses worn on the head, chains, stubble, lint: no source in the receipts isolates any of these. An engine that charged points for them would be inventing precision it does not have.
